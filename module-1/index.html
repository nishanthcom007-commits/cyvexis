Understanding the Threat & Emergency Response

Knowledge is your first line of defense. Here is what phishing actually is and exactly how to respond if you've already engaged with a suspicious link.

🎣

What is Phishing?

Phishing is a type of social engineering cyberattack where criminals impersonate trusted entities—such as Google, Microsoft, your bank, Netflix, or even your company’s CEO—to trick you into revealing sensitive information.

Attackers send emails, text messages (smishing), or instant messages containing links to counterfeit websites designed to harvest your passwords, credit card numbers, multi-factor authentication codes, or install malware on your device.

Key Takeaway Phishing doesn't hack computer systems; it hacks human trust, curiosity, fear, and urgency.
🚨

What To Do If You Clicked a Link?

Accidents happen! If you clicked a suspicious link or entered your credentials, act quickly without panicking:

1
Disconnect from the Network Immediately Turn off Wi-Fi or unplug your Ethernet cable to prevent malware from contacting external servers or spreading across the network.
2
Change Your Passwords from a Clean Device Do NOT use the potentially compromised device. Use your phone or another secure computer to change passwords and reset active sessions.
3
Alert Your IT / Security Team Immediately Always report the incident right away. Prompt disclosure allows defenders to revoke leaked authentication tokens and block the attacker's domain.
4
Enable / Reset Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) Ensure your account enforces MFA using an authenticator app (like Google Authenticator or hardware keys) rather than easily intercepted SMS.

Common Deceptive URL Tactics

Compare the deceptive fake link against the authentic legitimate destination.

🎭

1. Subdomain Masquerading

Attackers append a target brand name into the subdomain to fool users scanning quickly from left to right.

FAKE paypal.com.user-verify.net
REAL account.paypal.com
🔤

2. Typosquatting (Lookalikes)

Replacing characters (0 for o, 1 for l, or swapped letters) that easily slip past casual reading.

FAKE micros0ft.com / paypa1.com
REAL microsoft.com / paypal.com
🔗

3. Hyphenated Security Traps

Combining brand names with security words like "verify", "support", or "login" in the registered domain.

FAKE apple-id-verify.com
REAL appleid.apple.com
🌐

4. Top-Level Domain (TLD) Swaps

Registering known brand names under cheap or unregulated domain extensions (.xyz, .top, .link).

FAKE netflix.co / chase-bank.top
REAL netflix.com / chase.com
🪞

5. IDN Homoglyph Attacks

Using international Cyrillic or Greek characters that look identical to Latin letters in browser fonts.

FAKE xn--e1afmkfd.com (displays google.com)
REAL google.com
📦

6. Raw IP Addresses

Using direct numerical IP hostnames in emails to host phishing credentials or malicious payloads.

FAKE http://185.220.101.5/login.html
REAL https://auth.company.com/login
// Section 04: Core Habits

The "Before You Click" Protocol

Four golden habits to protect your credentials and data every day.

01

Read the Domain Backwards

Locate the first single slash (/) in the address bar. Look immediately to the left to see the actual registered domain and extension.

02

Never Trust the Padlock Alone

HTTPS only encrypts the connection; it doesn't guarantee who owns the server. Over 80% of phishing sites display the lock icon.

03

Navigate Independently

If an email warns of an urgent restriction or locked account, open a new browser tab manually and navigate to your known bookmark.

04

Verify Financial Requests Out-of-Band

If an executive or coworker requests gift cards or emergency transfers, call them directly on a known phone number to verify.
